# ai-note-taking-evaluation
# Critical Evaluation of Gemini Notebook for Academic Learning

## 1. Project Overview

### Purpose of the experiment

This project evaluates Gemini Notebook as an AI-assisted research, summarisation and note-taking tool in a university learning context. The experiment uses the *Global Sustainable Development Report 2023 (GSDR 2023)* as the primary academic source and focuses on preparing a sustainability and Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) briefing.

The experiment investigates whether an AI-assisted notebook can help a student understand a lengthy academic report, organise important findings, identify relevant claims and locate information that can be checked against the original source. It also considers why students must critically evaluate AI-generated responses rather than treating them as automatically correct.

The practical task involved reviewing the report's discussion of global sustainable development, inequality, conflict, progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), and transformation strategies. Gemini Notebook produced a structured response covering the report's main purpose, key challenges, progress towards the SDGs, recommendations and findings relevant to a university ESG briefing.

Five claims from the generated response were then checked against the original PDF. This provided a small, source-based test of factual consistency and terminology.

### Experiment details

- **AI tool evaluated:** Gemini Notebook
- **Academic context:** University research and preparation of an ESG briefing
- **Primary source:** Global Sustainable Development Report 2023
- **Evaluation approach:** Qualitative, source-based claim verification
- **Claims checked:** Five
- **Main evaluation areas:** Summarisation, organisation of information, source verification, relevance to the academic task, accuracy risks and responsible use

This was an exploratory experiment rather than a comprehensive benchmark of the tool.

## 3. Features and Capabilities Observed

The following capabilities were observed through the Gemini Notebook response used in this experiment. This section describes the functions demonstrated in the available output rather than claiming that every possible feature of the tool was tested.

### 3.1 Summarisation of a long source

Gemini Notebook generated an organised overview of the report's purpose and central themes. It described the report as an assessment of progress towards the SDGs and highlighted the need to move from commitments towards practical action.

This was useful because a lengthy report can be difficult to review efficiently when preparing an initial academic briefing. A structured summary provides a starting point for deciding which sections require closer reading.

However, summarisation necessarily involves selection. A summary may omit qualifications, details, counterarguments or contextual information that matter to the assignment. It should therefore guide further reading rather than replace the original report.

### 3.2 Organisation into themes and headings

The generated response separated the material into themes, including:

- The report's purpose and overall message
- Global challenges and crises
- Progress towards the SDGs
- Recommendations and transformation strategies
- Findings relevant to a university ESG briefing
- Selected claims and their verification status

This structure made the output easier to scan and helped connect the report's broad discussion with a practical academic task.

The benefit is primarily organisational: the student can identify possible briefing topics without first writing a summary from scratch. The student must still decide whether the selected themes are relevant, balanced and sufficiently supported by the source.

### 3.3 Identification of numerical and conceptual claims

The response included numerical claims about forced displacement, income inequality and sustainability reporting, alongside conceptual claims about transformation entry points and the phases of an S-curve.

This gave the experiment two types of material to evaluate. Numerical claims could be checked against the relevant figures in the report, while conceptual claims required attention to the original terminology and meaning.

### 3.4 Support for an academic workflow

The response helped identify possible findings for an ESG briefing. The subsequent PDF checks showed how an AI-generated summary can be combined with direct source inspection.

The tool's output was treated as a starting point for investigation, not as the final evidence. The original report remained the authority for determining whether the selected claims were supported.

## 4. Experimental Methodology

### 4.1 Procedure

The experiment followed a source-verification workflow:

1. Review the GSDR 2023 report through Gemini Notebook.
2. Examine the structured response produced by the tool.
3. Select five factual or conceptual claims from that response.
4. Open the original GSDR 2023 PDF independently.
5. Use the PDF search function to locate relevant terms, figures or phrases.
6. Read the surrounding paragraph to determine whether the evidence matched the claim.
7. Compare the original wording with the AI-generated statement.
8. Record the result and identify any qualifications or terminology differences.

The PDF's search function was used as a navigation aid. Finding a matching number or phrase was not considered sufficient on its own: the surrounding context also needed to be checked.

### 4.2 Verification categories

The following categories were used to interpret the results:

- **Verified:** The original report directly supported the selected claim and its meaning.
- **Supported with clarification:** The source supported the underlying idea, but the wording needed to be made more precise.
- **Not verified:** The source evidence was insufficient, contradicted the claim, or could not be located.

These categories refer only to the five claims selected for this experiment. They do not represent an overall accuracy score for Gemini Notebook.

### 4.3 Evidence collection

Screenshots were captured from the original PDF to preserve the passages used in the verification process. The screenshots show the relevant figures or conceptual statements in their surrounding context.

The evidence should be added to this repository and linked from the results section. The screenshots are important because they allow a reader to inspect the source passages rather than relying only on the experimenter's conclusions.

## 5. Detailed Claim Verification Results

### Claim 1: Forced displacement in 2021

**AI-generated claim examined:** The number of people forcibly displaced worldwide reached 89.3 million in 2021.

**Search and verification process:** The original PDF was searched for the figure “89.3 million.” The relevant passage was found under the heading “Rising levels of conflict war and instability.”

**Evidence in the original report:** The passage states that the number of people forcibly displaced in 2021 reached a record 89.3 million. It also lists 27.1 million refugees and 53.2 million internally displaced people.

**Result: Verified.**

The main figure and the year match the original report. The surrounding passage also places the statistic in the context of armed conflict and instability.

**Critical interpretation:** This check demonstrates the importance of reading the full paragraph rather than extracting a number in isolation. The two additional figures listed in the passage should not automatically be presented as an exhaustive breakdown of the total without checking the report's definitions and categories. The total is the principal claim verified in this test.

**Evidence file:** `evidence/claim-01-forced-displacement.png`

### Claim 2: Global income inequality

**AI-generated claim examined:** The richest 10% of the global population receives 52% of global income, while the poorest 50% receives 8.5%.

**Search and verification process:** The original report was searched for “52%.” The relevant passage discussing global income inequality was located, and both percentages were checked in context.

**Evidence in the original report:** The passage explicitly states that the richest 10% of the global population takes 52% of global income, while the poorest half earns 8.5%.

**Result: Verified.**

Both figures match the wording and meaning of the selected claim.

**Critical interpretation:** The statistic supports the report's discussion of global income inequality. However, these figures alone do not explain every cause or consequence of inequality. A wider academic discussion would require additional context and relevant evidence rather than treating one statistic as a complete explanation.

**Evidence file:** `evidence/claim-02-income-inequality.png`

### Claim 3: Sustainability reporting among S&P 500 firms

**AI-generated claim examined:** The percentage of firms in the S&P 500 releasing reports increased from 35% in 2010 to 86% in 2021.

**Search and verification process:** The initial search for “35% in 2010” did not locate the relevant passage. A search for “35%” also returned a different statistic relating to women's share of total labour income. That passage was not evidence for the sustainability-reporting claim.

The search was then refined to “transformation framework,” which led to a section discussing voluntary reporting of environmental, social and governance metrics. The relevant paragraph contained the exact comparison.

**Evidence in the original report:** The report states that the share of firms in the S&P 500 releasing reports increased from 35% in 2010 to 86% in 2021.

**Result: Verified.**

The exact statistic was located and matched the AI-generated claim.

**Critical interpretation:** This check illustrates why matching a number alone is not enough. The figure of 35% appeared in more than one context within the report. The first occurrence located concerned labour income, whereas the relevant passage concerned corporate reporting. Without checking the surrounding text, a reader could mistakenly use evidence about one subject to support a claim about another.

The search process also demonstrates that a failed initial search does not establish that a claim is false. The appropriate response is to refine the search, examine related terminology and inspect the source context before reaching a conclusion.

**Evidence file:** `evidence/claim-03-sustainability-reporting.png`

### Claim 4: Six transformation entry points

**AI-generated claim examined:** The transformation framework consists of six key elements.

**Search and verification process:** The original report was searched for wording related to the transformation framework. A relevant passage explicitly referring to “six entry points” was located.

**Evidence in the original report:** The report states that it proposes six entry points, described as closely related systems with opportunities to use synergies among targets and resolve or reduce trade-offs.

**Result: Supported with clarification.**

The source supports the underlying idea that six entry points are proposed. However, the report's precise term is **“six entry points,”** rather than the broader wording “six key elements.”

**Critical interpretation:** Terminology matters in academic work. Although “key elements” may communicate a similar general idea, it is not the exact phrase used in the source. A more faithful statement is that the report proposes six entry points for transformation. This verification supports that specific claim; it does not establish that the entry points are equivalent to every element of the wider framework.

**Evidence file:** `evidence/claim-04-six-entry-points.png`

### Claim 5: The S-curve nature of transformations

**AI-generated claim examined:** Transformations can be understood through three phases: emergence, acceleration and stabilisation.

**Search and verification process:** The original PDF was searched for “emergence, acceleration.” The relevant passage was located under the heading “S-curve nature of transformations.”

**Evidence in the original report:** The report describes a stylised model of transformation through three phases—emergence, acceleration and stabilisation—and states that the process traces an S-curve.

**Result: Verified.**

The three phases identified in the AI-generated claim match those in the original report.

**Critical interpretation:** This check supports the report's description of the conceptual model. It does not demonstrate that every real-world sustainability transition necessarily follows the model in precisely the same way. The source describes a stylised model intended to help understand transformation, so the claim should retain that framing.

**Evidence file:** `evidence/claim-05-transformation-s-curve.png`

## 7. Critical Evaluation of Usefulness

### 7.1 Efficiency and information organisation

The structured response helped organise the report's content into manageable themes. This is potentially useful during the early stage of an academic task, when a student needs to identify relevant topics before reading particular sections in depth.

The experiment did not measure the time saved, so it would be inappropriate to claim a quantified improvement in productivity. The observed benefit was organisational rather than a measured reduction in time.

### 7.2 Relevance to the academic task

The response identified material relevant to a university ESG briefing, including inequality, sustainability reporting and transformation strategies. These themes provided a basis for selecting claims to investigate.

However, relevance depends on the assignment question. A topic may be important in the source but not central to a particular assessment. The student must decide which findings to use and explain how they relate to the task.

### 7.3 Source-grounding and evidence quality

The five checks showed that selected claims could be traced to relevant passages in the original report. The process also revealed that a source search can produce a similar-looking number in a different context.

This makes independent verification a necessary part of the workflow. A reader should check the original wording, surrounding explanation and meaning rather than accepting a generated claim simply because it is presented confidently.

### 7.4 Support for critical reading

The verification process encouraged closer engagement with the original report. Instead of relying exclusively on the generated summary, the student examined the source, refined searches and identified a terminology distinction.

The tool was therefore most useful as a starting point for investigation. The final judgement depended on reading and interpreting the original evidence.

## 8. Limitations, Risks and Responsible Use

### 8.1 Potential hallucination or inaccurate wording

AI-generated summaries may contain inaccurate, incomplete or overconfident statements. This experiment did not establish that hallucinations occurred in the five selected claims. It did, however, demonstrate why checking the output is necessary before using it in academic work.

A larger experiment would be needed to estimate how frequently unsupported or inaccurate claims occur.

### 8.2 Risk of misleading matches

The repeated appearance of 35% in different contexts showed that a matching figure can be misleading if its surrounding text is ignored. A statistic about labour income cannot be used to support a claim about corporate sustainability reporting simply because both contain the same percentage.

The verification process should therefore assess both the numerical value and the subject to which it refers.

### 8.3 Loss of context through summarisation

A summary may leave out qualifications, definitions, opposing evidence or details that are important to interpreting a claim. This is particularly relevant when using reports that discuss complex social, economic and environmental systems.

The student should return to the original source whenever a claim is important to the argument, surprising, ambiguous or likely to influence a conclusion.

### 8.4 Bias and selective emphasis

A generated response may give more attention to certain topics than others. This experiment did not directly measure bias or compare the generated summary with a systematic sample of the entire report. Consequently, it cannot establish whether the response was biased.

A stronger evaluation would compare the AI-generated summary with a human-prepared summary, assess the balance of topics included and omitted, and examine whether competing interpretations were represented fairly.

### 8.5 Over-reliance on AI

Accepting a generated response without checking the source could lead to inaccurate statements, weak arguments or inappropriate academic referencing. Students remain responsible for the claims and interpretations included in their work.

The appropriate workflow is to use AI to assist with initial organisation, then read relevant source passages, validate claims, cite the original publication and write the final analysis in accordance with university academic-integrity requirements.

### 8.6 Scope and generalisability

The experiment used one report and five selected claims. It did not compare multiple AI tools, test all possible notebook features, conduct a controlled time-saving study or assess performance across different disciplines.

The findings should therefore be treated as an exploratory case study. They provide evidence about this particular workflow, not a universal conclusion about the accuracy or suitability of every AI note-taking system.

## 9. Improvements for a Future Experiment

A more comprehensive evaluation could include the following improvements:

1. **Test more claims:** Select a larger set of numerical, conceptual and recommendation-based claims from different report sections.
2. **Record the original prompts and responses:** Preserve the exact questions asked and the complete AI-generated answers so the experiment can be reproduced.
3. **Use a standard verification log:** Record each claim, page or section, supporting passage, outcome and explanation.
4. **Test unsupported claims deliberately:** Include claims that cannot be found in the source to evaluate how the tool handles missing evidence.
5. **Compare summaries:** Ask a human reader to produce a summary and compare the coverage, relevance and accuracy of both versions.
6. **Measure efficiency:** Record the time required for initial summarisation, source verification and preparation of the final briefing.
7. **Assess omissions and balance:** Check whether the summary includes the report's major themes and important qualifications.
8. **Maintain clear evidence records:** Store the original report link, screenshots and experiment notes alongside the README.

These improvements would make the evaluation more systematic and provide stronger evidence for comparisons or broader conclusions.

## 10. Conclusion

This experiment evaluated Gemini Notebook through a practical academic scenario: preparing an ESG and sustainable development briefing from the GSDR 2023 report.

The tool generated a structured response that helped organise key themes and identify claims for further investigation. Five selected factual and conceptual claims were then checked against the original PDF. Four were directly verified, while one was supported with a terminology clarification. The process also showed why searching for a number without checking its context can lead to a misleading interpretation.

The main conclusion is that AI-assisted note-taking can be useful for initial exploration and organisation, but its output should not be treated as a substitute for independent source reading. Reliable academic use requires checking original evidence, preserving the source's intended meaning, acknowledging uncertainty and applying critical judgement.

The experiment offers limited but practical evidence about one tool, one source and one workflow. Further testing would be necessary before drawing broader conclusions about accuracy, bias, efficiency or performance across academic subjects.
